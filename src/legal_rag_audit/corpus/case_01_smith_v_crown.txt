UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE SECOND CIRCUIT

Docket No. 24-8902-cv
Decided: March 14, 2024

ARTHUR SMITH,
Plaintiff-Appellant,

v.

CROWN LOGISTICS CORP.,
Defendant-Appellee.

Before: CHEN, WALKER, and RIVERA, Circuit Judges.

OPINION OF THE COURT

PER CURIAM:

Plaintiff-Appellant Arthur Smith appeals from a final judgment of the United States District Court entering judgment in favor of Defendant-Appellee Crown Logistics Corp. following a bench trial on claims arising out of a commercial maritime freight and supply agreement executed in early 2022.

I. PROCEDURAL AND FACTUAL BACKGROUND

The fundamental dispute in this litigation concerns the enforceability of a commercial freight carriage contract entered into by the parties on January 15, 2022. Under the terms of the agreement, Crown Logistics Corp. agreed to transport specialized industrial turbomachinery from a deepwater port facility in Liverpool to a manufacturing facility in New York. However, prior to shipment, both parties operated under the mistaken understanding that the cargo sub-assemblies met standard panamax dimension thresholds, whereas in fact the machinery possessed non-standard dimensional variances that rendered transit under the agreed shipping fee structure impossible under federal maritime shipping regulations.

The plaintiff claimed damages of £150,000 for breach of contract. Specifically, Appellant asserted that Appellee breached Section 4.2 of the procurement charter by failing to secure container allocations on the designated vessel MV Sovereign Star. Appellant maintained that as a direct and proximate result of this non-performance, his manufacturing line suffered catastrophic operational downtime resulting in losses evaluated at £150,000.

Appellee cross-moved for summary judgment and, in the alternative, requested judicial rescission of the contract on the grounds of fundamental mutual mistake as to the core subject matter of the contract.

Following extensive pre-trial proceedings, evidentiary hearings, and submission of expert testimony regarding maritime customs and contract formation, the matter proceeded to a bench trial before the District Court.

II. JURISDICTIONAL AND VENUE CONSIDERATIONS

As an initial matter, we address the threshold jurisdictional challenges raised by Appellant regarding the application of transnational contract law principles in federal court. Appellant contends that because the damages were denominated in British Pounds Sterling (£) and part of the negotiation occurred in London, foreign choice-of-law principles should dictate the standard for contractual invalidation.

We reject this contention. Federal courts sitting in admiralty and maritime jurisdiction apply general maritime law, supplemented by state contract principles where not inconsistent with federal maritime policies. See Standard Oil Co. v. United States, 267 U.S. 76, 82 (1925); Maritime Freight Services Ltd. v. Apex Global Line, 412 F.3d 104, 112 (2d Cir. 2005). Because the charter party was negotiated and performed in substantial part within United States territorial waters, federal maritime choice-of-law rules govern, directing us to apply general federal contract principles.

III. MERITS AND LEGAL ANALYSIS

Under established common law and maritime precedent, a contract is voidable or void ab initio where both contracting parties share a fundamental mistake regarding a basic assumption on which the contract was made, which has a material effect on the agreed exchange of performances. See Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 152; Harrison v. Ocean Transports Inc., 389 F.2d 441, 447 (2d Cir. 1968); Vance v. North American Shipping Co., 519 F.3d 88, 94 (2d Cir. 2008).

Here, the record establishes beyond cavil that both Mr. Smith and the executive officers of Crown Logistics shared an identical, mistaken belief regarding the physical dimensions and regulatory classification of the turbomachinery at the moment of contract execution. Neither party knew, nor had reason to know, that the cargo required specialized heavy-lift cranes and custom naval clearance that neither party had bargained for or scheduled.

The judge ruled that the defendant was not liable for the damages. In a comprehensive 45-page memorandum opinion, the District Court found that the mutual mistake went to the very essence of the agreement, defeating the consensus ad idem necessary for the formation of a binding agreement.

Consequently, no compensation of £150,000 was awarded. The contract was found to be void ab initio due to mutual mistake.

IV. DICTA AND OBSERVED PRINCIPLES

Even assuming arguendo that the contract had not been void ab initio, Appellant's claim for consequential damages of £150,000 would fail for lack of foreseeability under the rule of Hadley v. Baxendale (1854) 9 Ex. 341, as incorporated into modern maritime jurisprudence. See Transatlantic Financing Corp. v. United States, 363 F.2d 312, 319 (D.C. Cir. 1966). Consequential damages for downtime lost in specialized manufacturing facilities cannot be recovered unless the carrier was provided explicit written notice of such extraordinary risk prior to or at the time of contracting. No such notice was provided here.

V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court is AFFIRMED in all respects.
